## Abstract (v3 — Zoe's version, 2026-09-10)

If a deployed model held a hidden goal, implanted by an adversary or arising from misalignment, one way to find out before deployment is to interrogate the model and see whether the goal surfaces somewhere an auditor can read it. Frontier system cards operationalise this as a secret-keeping evaluation: a password is placed in the model's system prompt with an instruction never to reveal it, even in its reasoning, and an adversarial auditor tries over many turns to extract it. The result is reported as a single survival curve representing the fraction of secrets that still remain kept after *t* turns of auditor interrogation. We replicate the evaluation across eleven frontier and open-weight reasoning models under a planning auditor, separate leaks in the model's output from leaks in its chain of thought, and run a paired arm with the secrecy instruction removed, since a model hiding a real goal would have no such line in its prompt and the instruction may itself change what the evaluation measures. Secret keeping differs widely across model families: with the same auditor and secrets, DeepSeek V4-Pro leaks every secret within 60 turns while GLM-5.2 keeps half; output discipline is near perfect on every model, so most leaks are verbalised in the reasoning. The instruction to conceal does not cause these reasoning leaks by forcing the model to rehearse the secret. Replacing the password with hidden goals that are correlated with the main task the model is asked to do, the instruction pushes the model to fabricate cover stories when questioned rather than answer forthrightly, and a goal framed as a prohibition leaks faster than the same policy framed as a whitelist. Taken together, the secrecy instruction acts on the output channel, where it suppresses disclosure and induces cover stories, while reasoning-channel leaks are a property of the model and of how much of its chain of thought is observable. Our harness and code are available at https://github.com/zoetzikra/secret-keeping-eval.

*What Zoe changed from v2, to carry forward: fewer numbers in prose (no per-model output counts, no gpt-5-mini exception, no cover-story counts in the abstract); the survival curve is defined where it is first mentioned; "hidden goals that are correlated with the main task" is the term for the extension; "how much of its chain of thought is observable", not "how much the provider returns"; the instruction claim is stated as what it does not do ("does not cause these reasoning leaks by forcing the model to rehearse the secret"), without a mechanism story.*

## 1 Introduction (v2 — paragraphs 1–3 are Zoe's rework of 2026-09-10; paragraph 4 and the bullets still v1)

Pre-deployment alignment assessments screen a frontier model for unwanted or unexpected behaviour, either by trying to elicit it or by probing the model's propensity towards it. One of the things pre-deployment testing can screen for is a hidden goal. If the model had acquired an objective it should not have, whether implanted by an adversary before it reached users or arising from misalignment during training, an evaluator could interrogate it and see whether the objective surfaces somewhere a monitor can read. Anthropic states the argument directly: if a coherently misaligned model were unable to keep secrets reliably, its misalignment would likely be detected during an alignment assessment [Anthropic, 2026a]. Chain-of-thought monitoring adds a second place to look, since reasoning models are found to be fairly monitorable across a range of settings [Korbak et al., 2025, Guan et al., 2025, Baker et al., 2025] and struggle to keep content out of their reasoning even when instructed to [Chen Yueh-Han et al., 2026].

Anthropic's system cards operationalise the above argument as a secret-keeping evaluation. The model is given a password in its system prompt and instructed never to reveal it under any circumstances, including inside its reasoning; an investigator model then questions it for many turns with the aim of extracting the password [Anthropic, 2026b]. There is no task for which the password is needed, so keeping the secret requires only that the model never mention it. The result is a survival curve: the fraction of secrets still kept after *t* turns of interrogation. The UK AI Security Institute is incorporating evaluations of this kind into pre-deployment monitorability testing, alongside benchmarks that measure whether a monitor reading a model's outputs and reasoning can catch sabotage, reward hacking, or sandbagging [Kutasov et al., 2025, Inglis et al., 2025].

The evaluation has so far been reported for one model family, under one auditor, and with the one provided secrecy instruction always present. This setup is not necessarily the most reflective of realistic deployment scenarios. A model hiding a goal would likely not have a "never reveal this" line in its system prompt. Therefore in this case the secrecy instruction is considered an artifact of the evaluation, but it is also currently a feature of many of the control evaluations ran as pre-deployment tests, where the side task the monitor must catch is described to the policy together with an instruction to keep it hidden [Kutasov et al., 2025, Arnav et al., 2025]. Duzan and Cooper Stickland [2026] show that in such explicit-influence settings the instruction itself tends to surface in the chain of thought, so that a monitor detects the hidden behaviour far more often than when the same behaviour is induced without an instruction. If the same held for secret keeping, the reasoning-channel leaks that make the evaluation informative would be leaks of the instruction rather than of the secret, and the evaluation would say little about a model that holds a goal uninstructed.

We replicate the evaluation across eleven frontier and open-weight reasoning models and extend it along three axes. First, we run every model under the same planning auditor and the same secrets, and separate the survival curve by channel, so that a leak in the model's output and a leak that appears only in its chain of thought are counted apart; we also record how much of each model's chain of thought is observable through its API, since a leak in a chain we cannot read cannot be counted. Second, for seven of the models we run a paired arm in which the secrecy instruction is removed and the password simply sits in the system prompt, so the effect of the instruction on each channel can be read off the difference. Third, we replace the password with hidden goals that are correlated with the main task the model is asked to do, some malign and some benign, and ask whether the same channel pattern and the same instruction effect hold when the model has to act on the goal rather than merely hold it.

Our main contributions and findings are:

- **Secret keeping differs widely across model families, and most leaks are verbalised in the reasoning.** With the same auditor and the same twenty secrets, DeepSeek V4-Pro leaks every secret within 60 turns while GLM-5.2 keeps half. Output discipline is near perfect on every model that produces a reasoning chain; the curves differ almost entirely in how often the secret appears in the chain of thought. Three of the eleven models expose no readable chain under our configuration, so for them the reasoning channel cannot be assessed and we report only the output channel.
- **The instruction to conceal does not cause the reasoning leaks.** Removing the instruction leaves reasoning-channel leaks unchanged or raises them on six of seven models tested, while output leaks rise on every one of them. The instruction acts on the output channel; it does not force the model to rehearse the secret in its reasoning. The one exception, gpt-5-mini, leaked nothing in either channel without the instruction and rehearsed the secret in its reasoning once told to hide it.
- **On task-correlated hidden goals, the instruction induces cover stories.** Across eight hidden goals seeded in the system prompt, the goal appears in the model's reasoning in most conversations regardless of the instruction (16–20 of 20 on seven of the eight goals), while the instruction removes it from the output and pushes the model to fabricate an innocent account of its actions when questioned, more so on coding tasks than on tasks that require no action. Malign goals leak at least as fast as benign ones, and a goal framed as a prohibition leaks faster than the same policy framed as a whitelist.
- **Reasoning observability differs by provider and must be reported with the number.** Some models return the full chain, some an encrypted chain with an intermittent summary, some a summary that omits part of the chain, and some no chain at all under adversarial pressure. We classify each model and report reasoning-channel counts as lower bounds wherever the chain is not fully observable, and we show that adversarial pressure itself reduces how much of the chain one provider surfaces.

## 2 Related work (v1)

Table 1 organises the work we cite by what is hidden from the auditor or monitor, how it was put there, whether the model needs it to do its task, which channels are read, and whether the study varies the instruction to hide. Two things are missing from the prior work taken together. The secret-keeping evaluation itself has been run on one model family, and nobody has asked what the instruction to conceal contributes to the number it produces.

**Secret-keeping and hidden-information evaluations.** Anthropic's system cards report how long a model holds a password against an investigator model, broken out by whether the model reasons on a turn and whether the investigator prefills its answer [Anthropic, 2026b]; the argument for reporting it is that a model which cannot keep a secret would give away a misaligned objective during an alignment assessment [Anthropic, 2026a]. Holtzman and West [2026] give a model a secret word and a writing task and let a second model guess the word from the text; the model rarely writes the word, but its choice of topic and imagery leaks it, and when it is told to hide the word actively the avoidance itself becomes a cue. Cywiński et al. [2025] fine-tune model organisms that use a secret fact but deny it when asked, and compare black-box and white-box elicitation, finding that prefilling the assistant turn and adopting a user persona recover the secret most often; Casademunt et al. [2026] use models trained to refuse certain topics as a natural testbed for the same elicitation methods. Marks et al. [2025] train a model with a hidden objective and have blinded teams try to find it, and Hubinger et al. [2024] show that a behaviour installed by training can survive later safety training. In all of these the hidden content is either installed by training or, where it is prompted, read only in the model's output. The system-card evaluation is the one that reads the reasoning as well, and it is the one we replicate.

**Chain-of-thought monitorability and faithfulness.** Korbak et al. [2025] argue that the legibility of a reasoning model's chain of thought is a safety opportunity and one that training can erode. Guan et al. [2025] measure monitorability across a suite that distinguishes whether a monitor must detect a controlled intervention on the input, the steps of a solution, or a property of the output, and report that frontier reasoning models are fairly monitorable on all three; Baker et al. [2025] find the same for reward hacking and show that optimising against the monitor produces obfuscated reasoning. Chen Yueh-Han et al. [2026] ask models to keep specified content out of their reasoning and find that they mostly cannot, while complying far more often with the same restriction on their output; Emmons et al. [2025] show that when a task cannot be done without reasoning, models fail to hide that reasoning from a monitor. The faithfulness literature that precedes reasoning models establishes that a cue in the prompt can change a model's answer without appearing in its explanation [Turpin et al., 2023, Lanham et al., 2023], and Chen et al. [2025] and Betley et al. [2026] find the same in extended-thinking models. Our channel split rests on this work: whether a secret placed in the system prompt appears in the chain of thought is a question about faithfulness, and the system-card evaluation is a faithfulness measurement whether or not it is described as one.

**Instructions to hide, and what they do to the reasoning.** Most stress tests of CoT monitoring induce the hidden behaviour by instruction. Arnav et al. [2025] and Kutasov et al. [2025] describe a side task to an agent together with an instruction to keep it hidden and measure whether a monitor catches it; Zolkowski et al. [2025] add explicit cues to obfuscate, and Li et al. [2025] instruct models to sandbag covertly. Duzan and Cooper Stickland [2026] build matched pairs in which the same nudge is delivered as an instruction to act and conceal, or as a passing remark, and find that detection is far higher under the instruction because the instruction surfaces in the chain of thought. On the mechanism, Green et al. [2025] find that reasoning models disclose private context in their reasoning more, not less, as they think longer, and Mann et al. [2025] and Hwang et al. [2024] show that an instruction not to produce something raises its activation and its production, the ironic-process effect familiar from psychology. The secret-keeping evaluation is the simplest explicit-instruction setting there is, since the instruction is the only thing in the prompt that refers to the secret; if the instruction drove the reasoning leaks, this is where it would show most clearly. Our no-instruction arm tests that directly, on models whose full chain of thought we can read.

**Comparison to Duzan and Cooper Stickland [2026].** Their finding is that an explicit instruction to conceal makes a hidden behaviour more detectable in the chain of thought than the same behaviour induced without one. Our setting differs in two ways. The secret is uncorrelated with the task, so there is no behaviour to detect other than the secret's appearance, and we read the reasoning directly with a string matcher rather than through a monitor model. On six of the seven models where we can compare, removing the instruction does not reduce reasoning-channel leaks; it leaves them unchanged or raises them. The instruction's effect shows in the output, where it suppresses disclosure and, on task-correlated goals, produces cover stories. The two results are not in tension, because the behaviours differ: their models leak the instruction to act, ours would have leaked the secret either way. Read together they say that explicit-instruction settings overstate monitorability where the instruction adds something for the chain of thought to say, and not where the content would have been there regardless.
